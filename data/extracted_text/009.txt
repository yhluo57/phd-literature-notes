endurance in tunnel junctions having Mn-Ga electrode with high
perpendicular magnetic anisotropy

The composition dependence of the tunnel magnetoresistance (TMR) effect in Mn-Ga/MgO/CoFe
magnetic tunnel junctions (MTJs) for Mn54Ga46, Mn62Ga38, and Mn71Ga29 (at. %) electrodes was
investigated. An MTJ with a Mn62Ga38 electrode showed a maximum TMR ratio of 23% at 10 K
and high annealing endurance up to 375 C. The bias voltage dependence of the TMR ratio was
distinct among MTJs with different Mn-Ga compositions. Here, we discuss this dependence on the
basis of the difference in the D1 band dispersions for Mn-Ga alloys calculated by ﬁrst principles.

Magnetic thin ﬁlms with a high perpendicular magnetic
anisotropy (PMA) are essential for the development of spin-
transfer-torque-switching-type1
random
access
memories
(STT-RAMs), since these ﬁlms can aid the reduction of the
critical current required for writing a digital information bit,2
enhancement of thermal endurance,3 and improvement in
scalability.4 For STT-RAMs to be practically usable, magnetic
tunnel junctions (MTJs) should have good annealing endur-
ance because the standard backend technology for the metalli-
zation of complementary metal oxide semiconductor (CMOS)
circuits requires annealing typically at temperatures over
350 C. Among PMA ﬁlms for MTJs,5–9 D022-Mn3dGa
(Refs. 10 and 11) and L10-Mn-Ga (Ref. 12) ordered alloy thin
ﬁlms are attractive as they have high PMA (Ku ¼ 1–2  107
erg/cm3), a small Gilbert damping constant,13 and high trans-
port spin polarization14 and TMR ratio (expected theoreti-
cally).15 Moreover, it is preferable for MnxGa1x binary alloys
to possess thermodynamically stable L10 and D022 phases in a
relatively wide composition range of x  0.5–0.65 and
x  0.65–0.74,
respectively,
to
facilitate
device
fabrica-
tion.16,17 Then, the annealing stability of PMA in MTJs would
be expected to be high. While the structural and magnetic
properties of bulk Mn-Ga alloys have been studied exten-
sively, the dependences of the TMR effect on both the compo-
sition and the annealing effect have not yet been investigated.
Therefore, we investigated these dependences in this study.
All the ﬁlms used in this study were grown by an
ultrahigh-vacuum magnetron sputtering system (base pressure
<1 107Pa) with stacking structures of MgO(100) substrate/
Cr(40)/Mn-Ga(30)/Mg(0.4)/MgO(2.2)/CoFe(2.5)/Ta(5)/Ru(5)
(unit: nanometers). Three kinds of Mn-Ga compositions were
investigated:
L10-Mn54Ga46,
L10-Mn62Ga38,
and
D022-
Mn71Ga29 (at. %). All the layers were deposited at an ambient
temperature. In-situ annealing was carried out after the depo-
sition of Cr at 700 C, of L10-Mn62Ga38 and D022-Mn71Ga29
at 400 C, and of L10-Mn54Ga46 at 500 C. Each of the Mn-
Ga layers was subjected to post-annealing to promote a well-
ordered structure with good PMA properties. The correspond-
ing structural and magnetic properties were investigated using
an x-ray diffractometer and a vibrating sample magnetometer.
Each Mn-Ga layer exhibited well-squared hysteresis loops
and had high PMA (Ku ¼ 1–2  107 erg/cm3) as well as a
high degree of L10 ordering, which was weakly dependent on
the Mn-Ga the composition.18 Details of the magnetic and
structural properties of the Mn-Ga ﬁlms are discussed else-
where.19 The ﬁlms were patterned into pillar shapes with
sizes ranging from 10  10 lm2 to 100 100 lm2 by ultra-
violet lithography and Ar plasma milling techniques. After
the fabrication processes, MTJs were annealed in a vacuum
furnace at temperatures (Tanneal) ranging from 250 C to
400 C to improve the (001) orientation of the MgO barrier.
Electrical transport properties were investigated by a direct-
current four-terminal method at 10 K and 300 K. We calcu-
lated the electronic band structures using the commercially
available Atomistix ToolKit (ATK).20 This code uses numeri-
cal atomic orbitals and norm-conserving pseudopotentials.
We
employed
the
Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof
(PBE)-type
exchange-correlation functional.21
Fig. 1 shows typical dependences of the TMR ratio on
the magnetic ﬁeld perpendicular to the ﬁlm plane direction.
In the present MTJs, a completely anti-parallel conﬁguration
of the magnetizations of Mn-Ga and CoFe electrodes cannot
be realized, and the observed TMR ratio is underestimated
relative to that in the case of MTJs showing parallel to anti-
parallel magnetization switching (TMRP-AP). From the pres-
ent experimental results, TMRP-AP was obtained by an equa-
tion of the angular dependence of the TMR effect.22 The
detailed equation for the calibration is described in Refs. 16
and 23.

of
all

were 18% (Mn54Ga46, Tanneal ¼ 325 C), 23% (Mn62Ga38,
Tanneal ¼ 375 C), and 20% (Mn71Ga29, Tanneal ¼ 325 C) at
10 K. At 300 K, these ratios were 6% (Mn54Ga46,
Tanneal ¼ 325 C), 7% (Mn62Ga38, Tanneal ¼ 350 C), and 8%
(Mn71Ga29, Tanneal ¼ 350 C). The Mn62Ga38 MTJ exhibited
a higher TMR ratio than the others, although the magnitude
and the difference of TMRP-AP among the present samples
were small. However, we found a clear, different dependence
on Tanneal. Note that the annealing endurance of the
Mn62Ga38 electrode up to 375 C is higher than that of the
other PMA-material-based MTJs described in Refs. 5–9, and
the temperature is sufﬁciently high to be the process temper-
ature of the CMOS technology. One possible reason for the
high annealing endurance is the stable low-temperature
solid-solution phase of L10-Mn62Ga38,16 whose boundary to
the high-temperature phase is almost 700 C. On the other
hand, the other two compositions are relatively close to the
edge of other nonmagnetic phases, and their decomposed
temperature is relatively low.16,17 The temperature at which
TMRP-AP begins to decrease is lower than the disordering
temperature in the phase diagram for each composition. This
is attributed to the decrease in the disordering temperature
due to a strain24 at the Mn-Ga/MgO interface or the low
dimensionality at the interface.
Figs. 2(b)–2(d) show the dependence of the product of
the resistance and the area (R  A) in the parallel magnetiza-
tion conﬁguration in MTJs with Mn71Ga29, Mn62Ga38, and
Mn54Ga46 electrodes, respectively. For Mn71Ga29 (Fig. 2(b))
and Mn62Ga38 (Fig. 2(c)), R  A showed a slight decrease
with increasing Tanneal. A similar behavior was observed in
for the CoFeB/MgO MTJs.25 In contrast, R  A for the
Mn54Ga46 MTJs (Fig. 2(d)) decreased more drastically than
for the other two MTJs. Even though the reason for the dif-
ferent behaviors of R  A is unclear, the dependence indi-
cates the poor annealing stability of the Mn54Ga46 MTJs.
To better understand the dependence of the electronic
structure on the Mn-Ga composition, tunneling conductance
spectra (dI/dV – V; Fig. 3(a)) and the bias voltage dependence
of the TMR ratio of the MTJs (TMR – V; Fig. 3(b)) were
measured at 10 K. In these graphs, the negative bias region
corresponds to the tunneling of electrons from the top CoFe
electrode to the bottom Mn-Ga electrodes. The shape of the
dI/dV spectra for each composition is almost the same, which
is mainly attributed to the nature of the MgO barrier, as dis-
cussed in our previous works.15,23 The difference among the
compositions is apparent in the TMR – V curves. The TMR –
V curves in the positive bias side exhibited a similar shape,
because they were mainly affected by the electronic structure
at the MgO/CoFe interface. On the other hand, the negative
bias side exhibited an apparent difference, which implies
apparently different electronic structures at the interface of
the Mn-Ga electrodes. The TMR ratios of the Mn62Ga38 and
Mn71Ga29 MTJs decreased gradually with increasing magni-
tude of the bias voltage, whereas that of the Mn54Ga46 MTJ
decreased steeply near the zero-bias point. Such a steep
decrease is often attributed to the inelastic tunneling pro-
cess.26 However, the inelastic tunneling due to magnetic im-
purity is one of a possible factor, the calculated dispersion of
the D1 bands can also qualitatively explain the TMR – V curve
of the Mn54Ga46 MTJ, as discussed in the following section.

band seems to be small around the Fermi energy (EF) for the
L10-MnGa, because the D1 band touches EF in the minority
spin channel and crosses EF in the majority spin channel. EF
of L10-Mn54Ga46 is considered to be lower than that calcu-
lated for L10-MnGa (Mn50Ga50) because EF decreases with
decreasing total number of valence electrons under the
assumption of a rigid band picture. If EF is located just below
the edge of the minority D1 band, the tunneling conductance
from the minority spin channel is increased for the unﬁlled D1
states at a small ﬁnite negative bias, which is considered to
lead to a drastic decrease in the TMR ratio on the negative
bias side in the L10-Mn54Ga46 MTJ. The relatively gradual
decay of TMRP-AP in D022-Mn71Ga29 implies a gradual decay
of the D1-spin polarization for the minority band gap shown in
Fig. 4(d). The behavior of the L10-Mn62Ga38 MTJ is consid-
ered to be similar to that of the D022-Mn71Ga29 MTJ because
EF of L10-Mn62Ga38 is assumed to be located within the band
gap of the minority spin channel, if we consider the rigid band
picture. In this case, the spin polarization of L10-Mn62Ga38 is
higher than that of D022-Mn71Ga29. However, here, the reason
for the small TMR ratio in the experiments is still unclear. At
present, one possible reason is considered to be the imperfec-
tions (e.g., lattice dislocations, defects, and contaminations) at
the interfaces around the MgO barrier. In particular, a lattice
misﬁt of approximately 7% is observed between the Mn-Ga
ﬁlms and MgO, and such a large misﬁt may cause lattice dis-
locations and an unfavorable growth of the MgO barrier.
Imperfections at the interface were found by transition elec-
tron microscopy analysis of the D022-Mn71Ga29 MTJ, as
described in Ref. 15. To improve the TMR ratio, it is impor-
tant to choose some other barrier material or to reduce the
misﬁt by modifying the interface.
In summary, we investigated the TMR effect in Mn-Ga/
MgO/CoFe MTJs with ﬁlms of three different Mn-Ga compo-
sitions. Among the prepared samples, MTJs with L10-
Mn62Ga38 exhibited a high annealing endurance of the TMR
ratio up to 375 C and a maximum TMR ratio of 23% at 10 K.
The bias voltage dependence of differential conductance
differed slightly with varying Mn-Ga composition, whereas
that of the TMR ratio clearly depended on the Mn-Ga compo-
sition. The dependence in the TMR – V curve could be quali-
tatively understood by the dispersion of the D1 bands in the
Mn-Ga [001] direction. These experimental and theoretical
results suggest that a Mn-Ga ordered alloy with a Mn content
of about 60 at. % is promising from the viewpoints of achiev-
ing a stable alloy phase and high spin polarization.
